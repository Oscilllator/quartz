
# Apple in china
Good book. I had no idea that the way that China's manufacturing capabilities increased over time as they manufactured their way up the value chain was a deliberate policy from the beginning, rather than just the result of a natural learning process. I also didn't realise that Tesla in China was what kicked off their auto industry.
# Theft of fire
Good pulp fiction. I'll read the sequel, when it comes out.
# Termination shock
Not a bad book, but far from Stephensons best. I suppose having already heard about injecting sulphur into the stratosphere it had less of an impact. Love the idea of a sulphur powered pulse jet, though.
# Elephant in the brain
One of those books that I think would have had a much larger impact if I had read it earlier in life. Instead, it was merely a good book with some good points. It has occasionally prompted me to try to "shortcut" a mental path. If mental trait A exists to foster B, consider directly adopting B.
# The Box
Top tier classic, everyone seems to have read it by now. It updated me in the direction that unions can be quite effective. They did a great job of delaying the rollout of containers. Hearing the shenanigans going on in the port of LA, I am not surprised thanks to having read this.
# The dark forest
I now believe we should stop all radio emissions from earth. I'm serious.
# Most secret war
Top tier book. This is the kind of book that I wish more existed of - it is not a textbook, but neither is it a narrative of a series of events devoid of detail. If one wishes to learn about putting antireflective IR coatings on submarines to fool IR sensors during WWII, or how the progress of the V-1 bomb program was tracked, and how preparations were made for their arrival prior to any actually being launched at london, this is a book to read.

The author of this book also wrote "Instruments and experiences", which is another top tier book recommended by Dan Gelbart in the comments of one of his youtube videos. Recommended reading for the section on flexures in particular.

# Emberverse series
I quite liked this series. Like a few others in the same genre though it starts off excellent and kind of tails off as the series progresses. The first book or two are pretty good, but by the time they are traipsing across the land on a quest for a magic sword all pretense of science fiction has been lost. I stopped after book 7.

# From Third World to First
From the horses mouth on how Singapore was constructed. One must be careful taking in wisdom doled out with the benefit of hindsight, but nonetheless it's hard to avoid the impression that if all politicians were like Lee Kuan Yew we would all be living inside one of those AI generated cityscapes full of lofty spires, flying cars, and extensive tastefully arranged parks.

Mostly this impression comes from his ability to actually consider tradeoffs, something that is anathema to the modern politician. 
# 1632 series
The first book was excellent, the second good, and after that things devolved. The parts that explored the premise of the series, a slice of modern society transported back in time, were the good parts. However the author seemed to get bored of this and switched to entangling the various factions in increasingly convoluted political knots, which is not a genre I am a fan of.
# An Immense World: How Animal Senses Reveal the Hidden Realms Around Us
Popular science book that's worth skimming through. Contains some cool oddities like the Melanophila heat seaking beetle, and the four-eyed fish.

# Island in the Sea of Time, and the Nantucket series
Another series by S.M. Stirling. This one, perhaps as it is a trilogy, manages to stay on track. As such I recommend it more than the emberverse series. I like how in this one the antagonists aren't dumb also.
# Impro
I've heard a lot about this book, and have read most of it twice. It's certainly worth reading but I somehow haven't had quite the same revelatory experience claimed by so many others. This is perhaps because writing about Status explicitly has become more common in recent years, perhaps partly due to the (re)popularisation of this book.
# A Time of Gifts: On Foot to Constantinople From the Hook of Holland to the Middle Danube
A good fun read, but I confess I did not feel the need to finish it. I split ways about half way though Germany. I can only read so many stories about being taken in on cold winter evenings by good samaritans. Presumably having such events actually happen to you in person has a more lasting appeal.

# Exhalation (Ted Chiang) 
Quality book, good ideas. In the coming years we may see something like "The lifecycle of software objects" come to pass.
