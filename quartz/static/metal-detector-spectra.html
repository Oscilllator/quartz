What a metal detector hears, 10 Hz to 1 MHz

Response of a gold nugget, an iron sphere and magnetic, viscous, slightly salty ground across frequency. Below that, the noise floor of an air-cored coil versus a fluxgate. Drag the sliders. Hover any chart for values.

The quantity plotted is the response ratio: the field returned by the object, averaged over the receive coil, divided by the transmitter's own field at the coil centre, in parts per million (ppm). In-phase (I) is the part in step with the transmit current. Quadrature (Q) is the part lagging 90°; a lossy (resistive) response is plotted as positive Q. Phase φ = atan2(Q, I), in degrees.

Glossary: read this first
Primary field
The magnetic field made by the detector's own transmit coil, Bp (tesla, T). Read: Overton, §5 Induction, p.47 · Overton, Induction balance, p.49
Secondary field
The field that objects in the ground send back in response to the primary. This is the only thing the detector actually wants to hear. Read: Overton, Eddy currents, p.50
ppm
Parts per million: secondary field ÷ primary field at the receive coil, × 10⁶. A 1.3 g nugget whose centre is 15 cm below a 30 cm coil is roughly 0.2 ppm; the soil under it is thousands of ppm.
In-phase (I)
The part of the secondary field that rises and falls exactly in step with the transmit current. A magnetic material (pulls field in) gives positive I. A good conductor (pushes field out) gives negative I. Read: Overton, Phase demodulation (X/R channels), p.70
Quadrature (Q)
The part that lags the transmit current by a quarter cycle (90°). It comes from energy loss: resistance in eddy currents, or magnetisation that can't keep up. Plotted here as positive for lossy responses. Read: Overton, Phase demodulation, p.70
Phase φ
The angle of the response, φ = atan2(Q, I), in degrees. 0° = purely magnetic, 90° = purely lossy, 180° = perfect conductor pushing field out. Read: Overton, Ch.7 Target discrimination, p.69
Phasor
A single frequency's response drawn as an arrow from the origin to the point (I, Q). Its length is the amplitude; its angle is the phase.
Response size |M|
How big an object's response is at one frequency, regardless of phase: the length of its phasor, |M| = √(I² + Q²), in ppm. (M is the object's complex response, holding I and Q together.)
Eddy currents
Loops of current induced in a piece of metal by a changing field. The changing field (∝ frequency) drives them; the metal's resistance limits them. Read: Overton, Eddy currents, p.50
Time constant τ and corner frequency fc
τ (seconds) is how long eddy currents in an object take to die away after the field stops. For a non-magnetic sphere of radius a (m) and conductivity σ (siemens per metre, S/m), τ = μ₀σa²/π², where μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ H/m. A magnetic sphere (relative permeability μr) holds its currents longer: τ = μ₀μrσa²/q², with q between π and 4.49 (see the definitions at the bottom). The corner frequency fc = 1/(2πτ) (Hz) is where the quadrature response peaks. Far below fc the object is nearly invisible. Far above it the response is flat and says nothing about the material. Read: Overton, PI target response, p.146
Magnetic susceptibility χ
How strongly a material magnetises in an applied field (dimensionless, SI units). Air is ~0, quartz sand ~10⁻⁵, iron-rich soil 10⁻³ to 10⁻², pure magnetite ~3. Read: Ripka, §1.1.2 Field and matter, p.4
Magnetite, maghemite
Magnetic iron oxide minerals. Maghemite forms when iron-rich soil weathers in hot climates, and it is what makes Australian goldfield soil "hot". Read: Overton, Mineralization effects, p.60
Laterite, ironstone, hot rock
Laterite is deeply weathered, iron-rich soil. Ironstone is a rock cemented by iron oxides. A hot rock is a lump whose magnetic response differs from the surrounding soil, so the detector beeps on it as if it were metal. Read: Overton, Mineralization effects, p.60
Superparamagnetic grains
Magnetic grains so small (about 10–30 nm) that ordinary room-temperature thermal jiggling is enough to flip their magnetisation back and forth. Each grain flips on its own characteristic timescale, its relaxation time τN (s). Read: Ripka, §1.2.2 Ferromagnetism, p.11 (one-line mention only)
VRM (viscous remanent magnetisation)
In detector usage: the soil's sluggish magnetic response from superparamagnetic grains. When the field changes, the soil's magnetisation doesn't jump to its new value. It creeps there over microseconds to seconds, because different grains relax at very different speeds. "Viscous" means lagging, like syrup. In a frequency-domain detector this shows up as χ dropping slightly as frequency rises, plus a small constant quadrature. In a pulse detector it shows up as a slow 1/t decay that mimics a target. (The term is borrowed from rock magnetism, where it strictly means magnetisation slowly acquired over geological time. Detector engineers use it for the same relaxation physics on short timescales.) Read: Overton, PI ground balance: "magnetic viscosity", 1/t, p.174 · Overton, PI for nugget hunting, p.155
FD (frequency dependence)
How much the soil's susceptibility drops per tenfold (one decade) increase in frequency, in %. A practical measure of how much VRM the soil has. Ordinary soil is ~0–2 %; soil rich in fine maghemite is ~5–15 %. Neither Zotero textbook covers frequency-dependent susceptibility of soils.
Ground conductivity σg
How well the soil carries current (S/m). Dry soil is ~0.001–0.01; salty groundwater is 1–5. Conductive ground behaves like a huge, weak conductor, so its quadrature rises steadily with frequency. Read: Overton, Multi-frequency: notching out salt, p.193
Clutter vs noise
Clutter is unwanted signal caused by the transmitter itself, here mainly soil the detector failed to cancel. It grows with transmit power exactly as the gold does, so more power never helps against it. Additive noise exists whether or not you transmit: coil thermal noise, amplifier noise, mains hum, lightning. More power or more averaging time does help against it.
Soil patchiness v and model error ε
Sweeping the coil removes the steady soil background, so only variation in the soil across a target-sized patch can mimic a target. v (%) is how much each soil ingredient varies over that distance. ε (%) is the share of that variation that follows none of the model's soil shapes. Neither is known for real goldfield soil, and your library has no numbers for them; they have to be measured.
Depth falloff (1/z⁶)
The transmit field reaching a target falls as 1/z³ with distance z (m), and the field the target sends back falls as 1/z³ again, so the signal falls as 1/z⁶ once z is larger than the coil radius R. Doubling the depth costs 64×. Detection depth for a coin-sized target is roughly the coil diameter, and for a target at depth z the best coil radius is about R ≈ z. A bigger coil sees deeper but small targets less well. Overton, Coil sensitivity, p.196–197
Ground balance
What a single-frequency detector does to ignore soil: it measures only the part of the response perpendicular to the soil's phasor. This fails when the soil's mix (and so its phase) changes from place to place. Read: Overton, VLF detector with ground exclusion balance, p.87 · Overton, PI ground balance, p.174
Probe frequency
One frequency you pick with the slider (Hz). It stands for what a classic single-frequency detector running at that frequency would see. It sets the solid "probe" line on the charts, the dots in the phasor plane, the table, the two "Single tone" boxes and the sensor ratio box. The multi-tone fit does not use it.
Tone
One transmit frequency. A multi-tone detector sends several at once and measures I and Q at each. Read: Overton, Multi-frequency design, p.193
Least-squares soil fit
Find the amounts of the three soil ingredients (χ, the VRM slope Δχ, σg) that best explain the readings at all tones. Whatever is left over (the residual) is signal the soil can't explain, which is a target. Read: Overton, Multi-frequency subtraction, p.193
Noise density (T/√Hz)
A sensor's random noise expressed as an equivalent magnetic field per square root of measurement bandwidth. For example, 10 pT/√Hz averaged over 100 Hz bandwidth gives 10 × √100 = 100 pT rms. 1 pT = 10⁻¹² T; 1 fT = 10⁻¹⁵ T. Read: Ripka, §1.4.4 Noise, p.41
Air-cored coil
A loop of wire. Its output voltage is V = ω·Nc·A·B, where ω = 2πf (rad/s), Nc is the number of turns, A the loop area (m²) and B the field (T). The output grows with frequency. Read: Ripka, §2.1 Air coils, p.48 · Ripka, §2.1.1 Voltage sensitivity, p.51 · Ripka, §2.1.2 Thermal noise, p.52
Self-resonance
The coil's own inductance L (henries, H) and stray self-capacitance Cs (farads, F) form a resonant circuit at f = 1/(2π√(LCs)). Just below it the coil gets a free boost; above it the coil stops working. Read: Ripka, §2.1.3 Parasitic capacitances, p.53 · Overton, App. A Air-cored coil calculator, p.207
Fluxgate
A sensor that drives a magnetic core in and out of saturation at the excitation frequency fexc (Hz) and reads the external field from the asymmetry. It measures B directly, down to DC, but can only follow fields slower than about fexc/10. Read: Ripka, Ch.3 Fluxgate sensors, p.75 · Ripka, Sensitivity rules, p.88 · Ripka, Tuned output, p.98
White floor, 1/f noise
White floor: noise density that is the same at all frequencies. 1/f noise: extra noise that grows at low frequencies, below the corner frequency fk (Hz). Read: Ripka, §1.4.4 Noise, p.41 · Ripka, §3.9 Fluxgate noise, p.105

Positive means magnetic (pulls field in). Negative means eddy currents push field out. Read: Overton, Ch.7 Target discrimination, p.69

Quadrature Q(f)

A conductor peaks at its corner frequency fc. Viscous magnetic soil (VRM) gives a small constant quadrature. Salty, conductive ground rises in proportion to f. Read: Overton, PI target response, p.146 · Overton, Multi-frequency, p.193

Phase φ(f)

Ground sits near 0° at every frequency. Gold sweeps 90°→180°. Iron starts near 0° (magnetic) and only swings towards 180° far above its corner frequency; for a solid steel sphere that is above 1 MHz. Read: Overton, Table of target phase angles, p.70

Can a multi-frequency detector subtract the soil without subtracting the gold?

The problem. Over a nugget the detector measures soil + gold. The soil part is thousands of times bigger, and how much soil there is (and of which kind) changes from spot to spot, so it can't be measured once and subtracted forever. It has to be estimated from the same measurement that contains the gold. The danger is that the estimate absorbs the gold too: if some mix of soil could produce exactly the gold's signal, the detector subtracts it as soil and the nugget vanishes.

What this panel simulates.

  1. The detector transmits N tones (frequencies) spread evenly on a log scale across the shaded band, and measures I and Q at each: 2N numbers per spot.
  2. It models the soil as a mix of three known shapes: magnetic (χ, flat in-phase), viscous VRM (Δχ, sloping in-phase + flat quadrature), and salt (σg, quadrature rising with f). See the glossary. Two assumptions are compared. Mix known: the proportions of the three are known (from ground-balancing somewhere nearby) and only the total amount of soil under the coil is unknown, so there is 1 unknown. This is what a classic ground balance assumes. Mix unknown: all three amounts are free, so there are 3 unknowns. This is the honest case on patchy goldfield soil and hot rocks.
  3. It finds the soil amounts that best explain the 2N numbers (a least-squares fit) and subtracts that soil estimate.
  4. What remains is the residual: the part of the gold's signal that no mix of soil could imitate. That is all the detector can still see.

For the dots, the soil is assumed to follow the three shapes exactly and there is no noise, so only the gold needs to be fed through the fit: the dots show what of the gold survives the soil subtraction. The table below then adds back what this hides: the soil's departure from the model (clutter) and sensor noise.

The comparison table. Each cell is a ratio: the gold signal left after the soil fit, divided by whatever is left that could be mistaken for gold. Two things can be left:

  • Soil clutter: the detector sweeps and subtracts the running background ("motion mode", Overton p.92), so uniform soil cancels however badly it is modelled. What can hide a nugget is how the soil varies across a target-sized patch, set by "Soil patchiness v". The fit removes whatever part of that variation it can describe with its soil shapes. With the mix known, only a change in the amount of the same soil is removable, and any change in the mix leaks through. A further fraction ε of the variation follows no model shape at all. The detector compares what is left against the expected gold shape (a matched filter), so only clutter that resembles gold counts. The mix leak has a known shape and its resemblance is computed exactly. The unmodelled part ε has an unknown shape: by default it is treated as random, which over 2N measurements resembles gold by about 1/√(2N). The toggle switches to the worst case, where it is shaped exactly like gold. All of this is a response to the transmitter, like the gold, so transmit power cancels out of signal-to-clutter.
  • Additive noise: coil and amplifier noise from the sensor panel, averaged over the dwell time T. This does not scale with the transmitter, so more ampere-turns help here. Total transmit power is fixed and shared across the tones. Interference (mains, lightning, VLF stations) is also additive but is not modelled.

The big number combines both, 1/√(1/SCR² + 1/SNR²). The line underneath says which one limits. With the default settings soil clutter is the limit by a wide margin, so detection barely depends on power. In deep-target cases noise can take over: try a 0.5 m target with a small coil. Read down a column to compare one tone with many under the same soil assumption, and across a row to see what not knowing the soil mix costs.

Things to try.

  • Number of tones = 1. Nothing survives. One tone gives 2 numbers (I and Q) but the soil has 3 unknown amounts, so some soil mix can always match the reading exactly. A single-frequency detector can't tell gold from a change in the soil without extra assumptions.
  • Shrink the nugget until its corner fc is above the band. Inside the band its quadrature then rises with frequency, just like salt, and it gets absorbed.
  • Grow the nugget until fc is below the band. It then looks like flat negative in-phase, the same as slightly less magnetic soil, and it gets absorbed.
  • Conclusion: gold survives when the band brackets its fc, because only there does it show its peak, which no soil shape has. Wider bands keep more sizes of nugget visible.

Why "mix known" is a trap. With the mix known, even one tone keeps most of the gold. But that rests on the soil's proportions being exactly right under the coil. The last box above shows how small an error in the soil's phase produces a fake signal as large as the nugget. On the defaults it's a few hundredths of a degree. Real soil varies far more than that from spot to spot, which is why single-frequency detectors chatter on mineralised ground. In the "mix unknown" column one tone gives nothing (2 numbers can't pin down 3 unknowns), while many tones still recover the gold, without needing the soil to stay the same.

Why adding tones stops helping. The clutter and the gold are both measured on the same tones, so adding tones grows both together. Signal-to-clutter depends only on shapes: how unlike any soil mix the gold looks across the band (the "share" box), and how big the soil error is. Extra tones help only until the band brackets the gold's corner frequency and the soil shapes are pinned down; after that the limit is ε.

Sensor noise floor: field-equivalent noise Bn(f)

Lower is better. A coil's output is V = ω·N·A·B, so its noise referred to field falls as 1/f until the coil's self-resonance, then rises steeply. A fluxgate measures B directly with a flat floor down to near DC, but its bandwidth is borrowed from its excitation (≈ fexc/10). Limits of this evidence. No source here gives fluxgate noise above about 10 Hz, so the flat fluxgate floor out to fexc/10 is an assumption, and probably a generous one. The published coils are optimised instruments, mostly with ferromagnetic cores, not detector search coils. Read the gap as "hundreds to a thousand times at kHz", not as the model's exact ratio. Also, in a detector neither floor is the limit: ground and interference are thousands of times larger. In your own bench test at 8.5 kHz the coil's SNR (signal-to-noise ratio) was only 20× better, because interference, not sensor noise, set the floor.

1. The board

Grey lines are the streets (copper traces) and dots are the switches. The board has two identical grids: a transmit (TX) layer that carries the drive current, and a receive (RX) layer 1.6 mm below it that picks up the returning field.

The purple region shows a TX loop the switches have routed around an L-shaped block of cells. The green region is an RX loop somewhere else. Any region made of whole cells works, as long as its edge forms a closed path.

2. The key fact: every loop is a sum of single cells

=

Four adjacent cells, each carrying the same anticlockwise current, are identical to one loop around all four: along every shared inside edge the two currents run in opposite directions and cancel. So any loop the switches can make equals the sum of the single-cell loops it encloses.

The physics is linear, so every measurement the board can make is a weighted sum of one 256 × 256 table: the response with TX on cell k and RX on cell l, called Mkl. A configuration with TX pattern a and RX pattern b (each a list of 0/1 per cell) reads aT·M·b. That one fact explains all the results below. Switching configurations can't create new information beyond M. What they change is how much signal each measurement collects, and so how much noise ends up in the estimate.

3. The configurations compared

Why corner rectangles contain every single cell

−−+=

A single cell at (i, j) is the difference of four corner rectangles: R(i, j) − R(i−1, j) − R(i, j−1) + R(i−1, j−1). Every cell, and therefore every loop shape, can be rebuilt from the rectangle measurements, while each measurement itself used a large, strong loop.

3b. Why big loops can lose: two kinds of error

Every measurement is wrong by some amount, and there are two ways it can be wrong. They favour opposite loop sizes.

  • Additive noise (σ, volts): the receiver's thermal and amplifier noise. It is the same size however big the signal is. A big loop collects much more signal against the same σ, so big loops win. This is the "multiplex advantage", the reason the grid uses big loops at all.
  • Proportional error (ε, a fraction): errors that scale with what the loop picks up. Examples are a slightly wrong height or calibration (η, a fraction of the soil signal the loop sees), and drift in the direct TX-to-RX coupling (ζ, a fraction of the coupling, which is huge when TX and RX overlap and tiny when they're far apart). A loop that sees lots of soil carries a big absolute error even when the detail you want is small. Here small loops win.

The same effect at scale: estimate the signal s (volts) in each of N cells. Either measure each cell with its own small loop (N measurements, each with one cell's signal), or measure N big patterns that each cover half the cells (Hadamard-style) and solve for the cells. Assume the cells have similar signals. The error left on each cell is:

N cellsAdditive noise: small loopsAdditive noise: big patternsProportional error: small loopsProportional error: big patterns
4σ1 σ1× betterε·s2 ε·s2× worse
16σ0.5 σ2× betterε·s4 ε·s4× worse
64σ0.25 σ4× betterε·s8 ε·s8× worse
256σ0.12 σ8× betterε·s16 ε·s16× worse

Each big pattern collects about N/2 cells' worth of signal. Against fixed noise that is a gain of √N/2 per cell. But each big reading's proportional error is also about N/2 times bigger, and solving for one cell can't cancel it, leaving about √N times more error per cell. For the soil problem it's worse still: the detail you want (gold, or soil variation) is tiny next to the total soil signal every big loop sees.

What this means for the grid: when time is short, additive noise dominates and big loops (corner rectangles, multi-scale squares) win by a lot. When time is unlimited, additive noise disappears, the proportional errors are all that's left, and the small-loop set (all cell pairs) comes out ahead. The best real design uses big loops but keeps proportional errors small: measure height and pose precisely, and keep the TX–RX coupling stable. A height error that is shared by all configurations at one position is a single unknown the inversion can estimate and remove, so it hurts far less than independent errors; the simulations model height that way. What still penalises big loops is mostly the coupling drift ζ: a big TX rectangle overlapping a big RX rectangle has a very large mutual inductance, so even a 10⁻⁷ drift is a large absolute error. The simulation applies ζ to the full, un-nulled coupling. Nulling each configuration electronically before the ADC would shrink this error by the same factor as the null, and would favour the big-loop designs.

  • Fields: exact Biot–Savart fields of every trace, checked against the analytic field at the centre and on the axis of a square loop (exact match). TX–RX coupling uses exact mutual inductances between straight trace segments.
  • Soil and target response: first-order (Born) approximation. Here ω = 2πf is the angular frequency (rad/s) with f = 10 kHz; I is the TX current (A); μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ H/m; and Ba, Bb are the fields per amp (T/A) of the TX and RX loops. For uniform soil this agrees with the exact image-loop result to within 3%.
  • Clutter: the soil left over after the multi-frequency soil fit (explainer tab). It is modelled as a random susceptibility field in the top 21 cm, with standard deviation 10⁻⁵ SI (χ = 10⁻² × 10% patchiness × 1% model error) and correlation length 3 cm. It is represented by 11,716 voxels: 1.5 cm voxels in the top 6 cm and 3 cm voxels below.
  • Target: a gold sphere directly below the board centre, with 20% of its signal surviving the soil fit.
  • Hardware:
    • Traces: 2 mm wide, 2 oz copper; 50 mΩ per switch passed through.
    • Transmitter: TX current limited to a 10 W dissipation budget and at most 5 A.
    • Receiver noise: thermal noise of the RX loop resistance plus 0.5 nV/√Hz amplifier noise.
    • Conventional coil: 0.4 mm wire, 4.9 Ω per coil.
  • Time budget: a time T per board position, shared equally by all configurations, so each gets T ÷ (number of configurations). Switching overhead is ignored. "Unlimited" means T = 10¹² s, where electronic noise is negligible.
  • Errors that do not average away:
    • Board height: measured by radar to σh = 2 mm, one shared value per board position. The inversion estimates it, and only the 1% of its soil effect the multi-frequency fit leaves behind counts.
    • Per-configuration calibration error: η = 10⁻⁴ of each configuration's uniform-soil signal, independent between configurations.
    • TX–RX coupling drift: ζ = 10⁻⁷ of each configuration's mutual inductance.
  • Detection: the optimal linear detector, which weighs every measurement against clutter, noise and errors together. Mass detectable at signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) 5. Its numerics agree with an independent stable method to 10 significant figures.

6. What the results say

  • The conventional coil is purely clutter-limited. More time doesn't change its result (≈7 g at 5 cm, 129 g at 20 cm here). Extra measurement time is a resource it can't use, and the grid can.
  • The grid with no switching (B) gains nothing. It matches the conventional coil despite about 700× worse noise. All the benefit comes from combining many loop shapes, which lets near-surface soil be told apart from a deeper point target.
  • With realistic time (10 s per position), big loops win. Corner rectangles (E) are 11–13× better than the conventional coil at every depth, and multi-scale squares (C) 5–8×. All cell pairs (D) are noise-starved and about 10× worse than the conventional coil.
  • With unlimited time, the most complete small-loop set wins. All cell pairs reach 65–157× and multi-scale squares 51–126×. Corner rectangles stop at 21–31×, because big overlapping loops carry big proportional errors (section 3b), mainly coupling drift.
  • The hardware priority is keeping proportional errors small, and that starts with nulling the direct TX–RX coupling before the ADC. For overlapping loops that coupling is about 10⁶–10⁷ times the gold signal. An analog-to-digital converter's noise is a fixed fraction of its full scale: about 1 part in 1,000 for 10 bits, or about 1 in 16,000 for a good 16-bit converter (about 14 effective bits). If the coupling sets the full scale, the converter's own noise sits far above the gold, so the coupling has to be cancelled in the analog receiver first. A switched grid can't be balanced geometrically like a double-D coil. Each configuration therefore needs an electronic null: a stored, scaled copy of the TX current subtracted before the ADC. Whatever coupling is left must also stay stable, since its drift error is roughly (fractional drift ζ) × (residual coupling). Nulling shrinks that too. Next comes per-configuration calibration (η). Amplifier noise only matters when time is short, and a height known to 2 mm is enough once the inversion treats it as one shared unknown.
  • Grid resolution, edge effects and sensitivity are in the tables in section 5; conclusions on those will be added once all runs are in.

7. Caveats

  • Geometry only, one frequency. This is the gain from loop shapes. It overlaps only partly with the multi-frequency soil-fit gain, so don't multiply the two.
  • Ideal knowledge. The detector knows the clutter statistics, and the target sits at a known point directly below. Real soil isn't a smooth random field, and searching over position and depth costs a modest factor.
  • Error structure. Height is modelled as shared per position. η and ζ are still treated as independent in every configuration. Real coupling drift is probably partly common, for example with temperature; a common drift could be estimated and removed like height, which would help the big-loop designs.
  • Not modelled: electronic nulling of each configuration (would reduce the ζ error); transmitter current and phase noise, which reach the receiver scaled by the coupling; switch capacitance and charge injection; interference; nonlinearity; Earth-field motion; and acquisition time. Thousands of configurations take real time to step through.
  • Where the code lives: in a scratch directory. Ask for it to be copied into the repo before relying on it.

8. Detailed methodology

Everything the simulations do, written so the method can be reproduced or matched against the literature. Symbols are as in the table at the top of this tab; new ones are defined where they first appear.

8.1 Hardware model

  • Grid. Two identical planar grids of n × n square cells of pitch p = Lb/n, where Lb is the board width (m). The RX grid is at height h above the ground and the TX grid at h + 1.6 mm. A pattern is a 0/1 vector a ∈ {0,1}N, N = n², over cells. The switches route a single-turn current around the boundary of the selected cells. By superposition this equals the sum of the single-cell loops it contains, each carrying the same circulation.
  • Configuration. An ordered pair (a, b) of TX and RX patterns. Families used: all aligned squares of size 16/8/4/2 cells (multi-scale, rank 64); all single-cell pairs; the 256 corner-anchored rectangles R(i, j) = {cells (k, l): k < i, l < j}, which have full rank. Reciprocity lets only the pairs a ≤ b be measured.
  • Electrical. Loop resistance R = Pe·(Rseg + Rsw)·Nt, where Pe is the number of boundary edges, Rseg = ρCup/(w t) the resistance of one trace segment (trace width w, thickness t), Rsw the on-resistance of one switch and Nt the number of turns. TX current Ia = min(Imax, √(Pmax/Ra)).

8.1b Spectral stage (multi-frequency soil fit, explainer tab)

  • The detector transmits Nf tones and measures in-phase and quadrature at each: 2Nf real numbers per configuration. The soil is modelled as a linear combination of three spectral shapes: magnetic (χ, flat in-phase), viscous VRM with log-uniform relaxation times (in-phase ∝ −ln f, constant quadrature (π/2)Δχ), and conductive ground (quadrature ∝ f). These are fitted by least squares and subtracted.
  • The gold sphere response M(ω) (Wait 1951) is projected out of the span of those shapes. What remains defines meff (about 20% of the gold signal survives). The soil's departure from the three shapes, ε (1%), times its spatial patchiness v (10%), times χ₀ gives the residual clutter level σχ = 10⁻⁵ used in the spatial stage. Height-induced soil changes alter the soil's amount but not its spectral shape, so all but hres = 1% of them are also removed here.
  • The two stages are treated as separable: the spatial simulation runs at one effective frequency with these residuals.

8.2 Fields and coupling

  • Biot–Savart, finite straight segment from p₁ to p₂ carrying 1 A, at point r, with r₁ = r − p₁ and r₂ = r − p₂:
    B(r) = (μ₀/4π) · (|r₁| + |r₂|) · (r₁ × r₂) / ( |r₁||r₂| (|r₁||r₂| + r₁·r₂) )
    Cell field Bk(r) is the sum over its 4 edges; pattern field Ba(r) = Nt Σk ak Bk(r).
  • Mutual inductance between straight parallel filaments (Neumann, closed form; perpendicular filaments contribute zero). Segment 1 runs from 0 to ℓ₁ along an axis; segment 2 runs from s to s + ℓ₂ parallel to it, at perpendicular distance d⊥. With f(x) = x·asinh(x/d⊥) − √(x² + d⊥²):
    M = ±(μ₀/4π) [ f(s+ℓ₂) − f(s+ℓ₂−ℓ₁) − f(s) + f(s−ℓ₁) ]
    Cell–cell matrix Mkl between the TX and RX layers; pattern coupling Mab = Nt² aT M b.
  • Response to a small magnetic volume (first-order Born / reciprocity). A volume dV (m³) with susceptibility χ at r changes the RX voltage for configuration (a, b) by
    δVab = (ω Ia / μ₀) · χ dV · Ba(r)·Bb(r)
    The sensitivity kernel Ba·Bb is what the 3D view colours.
  • Target. A conducting sphere of radius as (m) acts as a dipole with strength q = 4π as³ meff, giving gab = (ω Ia/μ₀) q Ba(rt)·Bb(rt) at the target position rt. Here meff = 0.2 is a single effective value standing for the gold's response surviving the multi-frequency soil fit. The frequency dependence is the Wait (1951) sphere response, used in the explainer tab.

8.3 Soil, clutter and errors

  • Clutter. The residual susceptibility after spectral soil cancelling is a zero-mean Gaussian random field with covariance C(r, r') = σχ² exp(−|r − r'|/ℓ). It is discretised on voxels v with volumes dVv, and factored as Σ = L LT (Cholesky). Clutter on the measurements is G w with w ~ N(0, I), where G(ab),v = (ω Ia/μ₀) dVv Ba(rv)·Bb(rv) and the clutter field is Lw.
  • Uniform-soil response S0ab = χ₀ Σv G(ab),v + (the same sum over coarse deep layers to 0.9 m), where χ₀ is the background susceptibility.
  • Shared height error. One unknown per board position p, prior standard deviation σh. Its effect on the measurements is the column cp = hres σh ∂S0/∂h, computed by finite difference over dh = 0.1 mm, and it is estimated jointly with the clutter. Here hres = 1% is the share of the height-induced soil change that the multi-frequency soil fit cannot remove.
  • Independent noise per configuration (variance, V²). τ = T/Ncfg is the time per configuration (s), ν the analog null factor, BADC the ADC's effective bits and fs its sample rate (Hz):
    σab² = (4kBTKRb + en²)/τ + (ζ ω Ia |Mab| ν)² + (η S0ab)² + [ (ω Ia|Mab|ν / SNRADC)² / (fs/2) / τ ]
    with SNRADC = 10(6.02 BADC + 1.76)/20. The ADC term is included only in the ADC runs.

8.4 Detector and figure of merit

  • Measurement model: y = α g + G w + Σp θp cp + n, with w ~ N(0, I), θp ~ N(0, 1), n ~ N(0, D), D = diag(σab²), and α the target amplitude.
  • The optimal linear detector (prewhitened matched filter, i.e. generalised least squares against Gaussian clutter plus noise) has
    SNR² = gT (A AT + D)−1 g, A = [ G L, c1 … cP ]
  • Evaluated in the low-dimensional unknown space with the Woodbury identity. With H = GTD−1G, hg = GTD−1g, hc = GTD−1c, K = [[LTHL, LThc], [hcTL, cTD−1c]] and u = [LThg; cTD−1g]:
    SNR² = gTD−1g − uT (I + K)−1 u
  • Minimum detectable mass: SNR scales with the sphere's volume, so mmin = (5 / SNR1 g) × 1 g at threshold SNR = 5. The gain of a system is mmin(conventional coil) / mmin(system) in the same run.

8.5 Algorithm (pseudocode)

for each system S (conventional coil | grid family):
    patterns P_tx, P_rx;  configs = pairs (a, b), a ≤ b if reciprocal
    I_a = min(I_max, sqrt(P_max / R_a));   tau = T_pos / len(configs)
    H = 0 (nv × nv);  h_g = 0;  h_c = 0;  c·c = 0;  c·g = 0;  g·g = 0
    for each board position p in 3 × 3 sweep:
        B_k(voxels), B_k(target), B_k(voxels, h + dh)    # cell fields, cached
        B_a = Σ_k a_k B_k  for every pattern            # pattern fields
        for each block of configs (a, b):
            G   = (ω I_a / μ0) · dV · (B_a · B_b)(voxels)   # rows × voxels
            S0  = χ0 · (row-sum of G + deep-layer term)
            g   = (ω I_a / μ0) · q · (B_a · B_b)(target)
            c   = h_res · σ_h · (S0(h + dh) − S0) / dh
            var = (4kTR_b + e_n²)/tau + (ζ ω I_a |M_ab| ν)² + (η S0)² [+ ADC]
            W   = 1 / sqrt(var);  G, g, c ← W · (G, g, c)
            H  += Gᵀ G;  h_g += Gᵀ g;  h_c[p] += Gᵀ c       # GPU, fp32
            g·g += Σ g²;  c·c[p] += Σ c²;  c·g[p] += Σ c g
    K = [[Lᵀ H L, Lᵀ h_c], [h_cᵀ L, diag(c·c)]];  u = [Lᵀ h_g; c·g]   # CPU, fp64
    SNR² = g·g − uᵀ (I + K)⁻¹ u;   m_min = 5 / SNR_1g grams

8.6 Numerical implementation and checks

  • Cell fields agree with the analytic field at the centre and on the axis of a square loop to machine precision. The Born uniform-soil response agrees with the exact image-loop result to 3%.
  • The Woodbury evaluation agrees with an independent QR-based residual (stable least squares) to 10 significant figures.
  • The fast CPU code reproduces the original code exactly. On the GPU, accumulating H, hg and hc in fp32 with the projection and solve in fp64 matches all-fp64 to about 5 × 10⁻⁶ relative. Every run prints the cancellation ratio gTD−1g / SNR² as a guard; it stayed below about 10³, far within fp32's range.
  • Discretisation: voxels 1.5 cm (top 6 cm) and 3 cm (to 21 cm) in the main runs. The board-size runs use 3 cm voxels to 21 cm and 6 cm voxels to 45 cm, a soil domain of half-width Lb/2 + Lb/3 + 15 cm, and a 3 × 3 sweep with steps of Lb/3.
  • Board sweep: 3 × 3 positions; each position contributes its own block of measurements, and the clutter field is shared across positions. That is what turns the sweep into a synthetic aperture.

8.7 Terms for a literature search

The pieces of this method each have established names. The combination (a switch-reconfigurable coil matrix used as a multi-static electromagnetic induction array, with a joint inversion for near-surface soil clutter, for small-target detection) is what to search for.

  • Hardware: reconfigurable / switchable coil matrix; multi-static or MIMO electromagnetic induction (EMI) array; magnetic induction tomography (MIT); coil arrays for UXO classification (e.g. TEMTADS, MetalMapper); induction-balance coil design; programmable bucking/nulling of TX–RX coupling.
  • Physics: Born approximation for EMI with magnetic soil; sensitivity kernel / Fréchet derivative BTX·BRX; reciprocity theorem for coils; magnetic polarizability of a conducting sphere (Wait 1951); viscous remanent magnetisation (VRM) and frequency-dependent susceptibility of soils; soil compensation for metal detectors.
  • Signal processing: generalised least squares; prewhitened matched filter in correlated Gaussian clutter; detection in clutter with a Gaussian-process prior; Woodbury identity; nuisance-parameter marginalisation; Hadamard / multiplex (Fellgett) advantage and its loss under signal-proportional noise; optimal experimental design for linear inverse problems.
  • Analogies: MRI receive-coil arrays and parallel imaging (SENSE, g-factor); synthetic-aperture and MIMO radar; electrical impedance tomography.
